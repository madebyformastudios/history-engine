=== Part 1: The first kingdoms ===

Cleopatra lived closer in time to the Moon landing than to the building of the Great Pyramid. By the time she ruled Egypt, the pyramids were already about 2,500 years old, and Egypt was older still.

Almost everyone in Egypt lives on about four percent of its land. The rest is desert. That thin green strip along the Nile is where this story happens. It runs from the first farming villages, through pharaohs, Greeks, Romans, caliphs, sultans and kings, to a country of more than 106 million people.

Around fifty-five hundred BC, small farming settlements began to spread along the Nile. Every summer the river flooded and left behind a layer of black mud, so farmers could grow wheat and barley in the middle of the desert. The Egyptians called their land Keh-met, the black land.

Over time these villages grew into two kingdoms. Upper Egypt in the south, along the narrow valley, and Lower Egypt in the north, in the wide delta where the Nile meets the Mediterranean.

Around thirty-one hundred BC, a king named Narmer claimed both. He is shown on a carved stone palette wearing the white crown of the south on one side and the red crown of the north on the other. For the next three thousand years, every pharaoh would call himself Lord of the Two Lands.

The period we now call the Old Kingdom began around twenty-six eighty-six BC. Its kings ruled from Memphis, near modern Cairo, and they spent enormous resources preparing for the afterlife.

For King Joser, an official named Imhotep stacked six stone platforms on top of each other at Sakkara. The Step Pyramid was the first large building in Egypt made entirely of stone.

Within about a century, Egyptian builders had learned to make the sides smooth. Around twenty-six hundred BC, the pharaoh Koofoo built the Great Pyramid of Giza. It was 146 meters tall and made of roughly two point three million stone blocks. For more than 3,700 years, nothing built by humans was taller.

Greek writers later claimed slaves built it. Archaeologists have since found the camps where the workers lived, and they point to Egyptian laborers called up for service, probably around 13,000 at a time and up to 40,000 at the peak.

Then the floods failed. In the twenty-second century BC, a long drought kept the Nile low for years. Harvests collapsed, and so did the central government. Local governors took over, fought each other, and for about two hundred years Egypt was split apart.

Around twenty thirty BC, Mentuhotep the Second, a ruler from Thebes in the south, reunited the country and began what we call the Middle Kingdom.

But around sixteen fifty BC, foreigners from the Levant took control of the north. The Egyptians called them the Hicksos, rulers of foreign lands. From their capital Avaris in the delta, they ruled Lower Egypt for about a century. They brought horses with them, and possibly new weapons as well.

=== Part 2: Empire and decline ===

Around fifteen fifty BC, Ahmose of Thebes drove the Hicksos out. Egypt now entered the New Kingdom, and this time it pushed far beyond its deserts and built an empire.

Hat-shep-soot, one of the few women to rule as pharaoh, sent a trading expedition to the distant Land of Punt and filled the temples at Karnak with new buildings. Her stepson Thutmose the Third fought at least sixteen campaigns in twenty years and captured some 350 cities. At its height, his empire reached from the Euphrates in Syria to deep into Nubia in the south.

A few generations later, Akhenaten pushed aside Egypt's many gods and built the state religion around one: the Aten, the disc of the sun. He built a new capital in the desert, and Egyptian art suddenly looked different.

After his death, the young Tutankhamun, probably his son, went back to the old gods and the old capital. Tutankhamun died at about 18 and was buried in a small tomb that robbers mostly missed. When Howard Carter opened it in nineteen twenty-two, he found more than five thousand objects inside.

The most powerful pharaoh of the New Kingdom was Ramesses the Second, who ruled for 66 years. In twelve seventy-four BC, he fought the Hittites at Kadesh in Syria, in what may have been the largest chariot battle in history, with five to six thousand chariots. Neither side really won.

Sixteen years later, Ramesses and the new Hittite king, Hattusili the Third, signed a peace treaty. The original was engraved on silver. It is the oldest international peace treaty known to historians, and a replica hangs today at the United Nations.

Around eleven seventy-seven BC, the eastern Mediterranean fell apart. As cities burned and trade broke down, raiders known as the Sea Peoples attacked Egypt. Ramesses the Third beat them on land and at sea, but Egypt never recovered its strength. By ten sixty-nine BC, the New Kingdom was over.

Over the next seven centuries, Egypt was ruled more and more often by outsiders.

Libyan dynasties ruled from the delta. Then the kings of Kush, from what is now Sudan, marched north. Their king Pee-yeh conquered Egypt around seven forty-seven BC, and for nearly a century these Kushite pharaohs, often called the Black Pharaohs, ruled the whole Nile valley.

They were pushed out by the Assyrians, the most feared army of the age. In six seventy-one BC the Assyrians took Memphis, and in six sixty-three BC they sacked Thebes.

Egypt won back its freedom for a while under Psamtik the First. But in five twenty-five BC, the Persian king Cambyses the Second invaded, and Egypt became a province of the Persian Empire.

=== Part 3: Greeks, Romans and Arabs ===

In three thirty-two BC, Alexander the Great arrived, and the Egyptians welcomed him as a liberator from Persian rule. On the coast he founded a new city and named it after himself: Alexandria.

When Alexander died, one of his generals, Ptolemy, took Egypt. In three oh five BC he declared himself king and started the last dynasty of ancient Egypt, which would rule for nearly three hundred years. The Ptolemies were Greek-speaking Macedonians, and their capital Alexandria became one of the largest cities of the ancient world.

They built the Great Library of Alexandria and a lighthouse on the island of Pharos, around 140 meters tall, counted among the Seven Wonders of the World. In one ninety-six BC, priests issued a decree for Ptolemy the Fifth in three scripts: hieroglyphs, Demotic and Greek. One copy, carved on a slab of stone, would later become the key to reading hieroglyphs.

The last ruler of the dynasty was Cleopatra the Seventh. She allied herself first with Julius Caesar and then with Mark Antony. In thirty-one BC, Octavian, the future emperor Augustus, defeated their fleet at Actium. A year later, in thirty BC, Cleopatra died, and Egypt became the personal property of the Roman emperor.

Under Rome, Egypt was the richest province outside Italy. Its grain fed the city of Rome, and Alexandria remained a center of learning.

Christianity reached Egypt early, and Alexandria became one of its great centers. Egyptian Christians, known today as Copts, built one of the oldest churches in the world. Their language, Coptic, was the last stage of the ancient Egyptian language.

In the seventh century, Egypt was caught between two exhausted empires. The Persians occupied it from six eighteen to six twenty-eight. A decade later, a new power arrived from Arabia.

In December six thirty-nine, the Arab general Amr ibn al-As crossed into Egypt with about 4,000 men. Within three years, the Byzantine fortress of Babylon and the city of Alexandria had both surrendered. Amr built a new capital next to the old fortress, called Foostaht. Egypt has been under Muslim rule ever since, although most Egyptians converted to Islam and switched to Arabic slowly, over several centuries.

=== Part 4: Cairo, the Mamluks and Napoleon ===

In nine sixty-nine, a Shia dynasty from North Africa, the Fatimids, conquered Egypt and founded a new city next to Foostaht. They called it al-Kahira, the Victorious. We know it as Cairo. They also built the al-Azhar mosque, which grew into one of the oldest universities in the world.

The Fatimids were brought down from the inside. Their Kurdish vizier, Saladin, took power in eleven seventy-one. From Egypt he fought the Crusaders, and in eleven eighty-seven he captured Jerusalem.

Saladin's successors relied on mamluks: soldiers bought as slaves, trained from childhood, and then freed to serve as an elite army. In twelve fifty the French king Louis the Ninth invaded Egypt on crusade. The mamluks defeated his army and captured the king himself, and France had to pay a ransom of 800,000 bezants to get him back. That same year, they killed the last sultan of Saladin's family and took the throne for themselves.

Ten years later, in twelve sixty, the Mamluks defeated the Mongols at Ayn Jaloot in Palestine. It was one of the first major defeats the Mongol armies had suffered. In twelve ninety-one they took Akko, the last big Crusader stronghold in the Holy Land.

Mamluk Cairo was one of the great cities of the Islamic world. Then, in thirteen forty-seven, the Black Death arrived, and plague returned again and again over the following decades.

In fifteen seventeen, the Ottoman sultan Selim the First defeated the Mamluks outside Cairo and hanged the last Mamluk sultan at one of the city's gates. For almost three centuries, Egypt was a province of the Ottoman Empire, though the Mamluk families stayed rich and powerful underneath.

In seventeen ninety-eight, Napoleon Bonaparte landed at Alexandria with an army and 167 scientists and scholars. On the twenty-first of July he defeated the Mamluks at the Battle of the Pyramids, but eleven days later the British admiral Nelson destroyed his fleet at the Battle of the Nile.

The French were trapped, but the scholars kept working. Their survey, published as the Description de l'Égypte, helped start the modern study of ancient Egypt. In seventeen ninety-nine, their soldiers dug up the stone with the decree of Ptolemy the Fifth near the town of Rosetta. Napoleon left Egypt the same year, his army surrendered in eighteen oh one, and the Rosetta Stone went to the British Museum. In eighteen twenty-two, the French scholar Shampolyon used it to read hieroglyphs for the first time in about fourteen hundred years.

=== Part 5: Muhammad Ali and the British ===

In the chaos the French left behind, an Albanian officer in the Ottoman army took control. His name was Muhammad Ali, and in eighteen oh five he became governor of Egypt.

In eighteen eleven, he invited the Mamluk leaders to a celebration at the Citadel of Cairo, closed the gates, and had them killed. Then he rebuilt the country, with a modern army, state factories and new schools, and turned Egypt into a major exporter of cotton. His armies conquered Sudan and parts of Arabia and Syria, and his son nearly reached Constantinople. In eighteen forty, the European powers forced him to pull back, but his family won the right to rule Egypt.

His grandson Ismail spent heavily to modernize Egypt along European lines. In eighteen sixty-nine, during his reign, the Suez Canal opened after ten years of digging. At 193 kilometers long, it connected the Mediterranean to the Red Sea, so ships from Europe no longer had to sail around Africa.

It also ruined Egypt's finances. In eighteen seventy-five, deep in debt, Ismail sold Egypt's share in the canal to Britain for four million pounds. Seven years later, after an army revolt, British troops invaded and defeated the Egyptian army at Tel el-Kebir. Britain controlled Egypt from then on, even though on paper it was still part of the Ottoman Empire.

When the Ottomans joined the First World War, Britain made Egypt a formal protectorate. In nineteen nineteen the country rose up in revolution, and on the twenty-eighth of February, nineteen twenty-two, Britain declared Egypt independent, while keeping control of defense and the canal.

That same year, Howard Carter opened the tomb of Tutankhamun, which set off a new wave of fascination with ancient Egypt around the world.

=== Part 6: Modern Egypt ===

On the twenty-third of July, nineteen fifty-two, a group of army officers called the Free Officers overthrew King Farouk. A year later the monarchy was gone, and in nineteen fifty-six one of the officers, Gamal Abdel Nasser, became president.

The last British soldiers had left the canal zone in June nineteen fifty-six. A month later, Nasser nationalized the Suez Canal. Israel, Britain and France invaded, but the United States and the Soviet Union forced them to withdraw, and Nasser came out of the crisis as the hero of the Arab world. In nineteen fifty-eight Egypt even merged with Syria into one country, the United Arab Republic, although the union broke up after three years. At home, after the United States withdrew its funding, Nasser turned to the Soviet Union to build the Aswan High Dam, which ended the yearly Nile flood that had shaped Egypt since before the pharaohs.

In nineteen sixty-seven, Israel destroyed Egypt's air force in a few hours and took the Sinai Peninsula and Gaza in six days. Nasser died three years later.

His successor, Anwar Sadat, attacked on the sixth of October, nineteen seventy-three. Egyptian troops crossed the Suez Canal and broke through the Israeli defenses on the other side. The war ended without a clear winner, but it gave Sadat the strength to negotiate. In nineteen seventy-seven he flew to Jerusalem, and on the twenty-sixth of March, nineteen seventy-nine, Egypt became the first Arab country to sign a peace treaty with Israel. Egypt got the Sinai back, and Sadat won the Nobel Peace Prize.

Much of the Arab world saw the treaty as a betrayal, and Egypt was suspended from the Arab League for ten years. On the sixth of October, nineteen eighty-one, eight years to the day after the crossing, Islamist soldiers shot Sadat during a military parade.

His vice president, Hosni Mubarak, ruled for the next thirty years.

On the twenty-fifth of January, twenty eleven, National Police Day, protesters against police brutality filled Tahrir Square in Cairo. After eighteen days and more than 800 deaths, Mubarak resigned. In twenty twelve, Egyptians elected Mohamed Morsi of the Muslim Brotherhood in the country's first free presidential election.

A year later, after huge protests against him, the army removed Morsi. On the fourteenth of August, twenty thirteen, security forces cleared his supporters' camps in Cairo, and hundreds were killed. The general who led the takeover, Abdel Fattah el-Sisi, was elected president in twenty fourteen and is now in his third term.

Today Egypt has more than 106 million people, and it still lives from the river. The Nile provides about 97 percent of its fresh water. In September twenty twenty-five, Ethiopia opened a giant dam on the Blue Nile, upstream, and Egypt protested to the UN Security Council. The Suez Canal, which carries around 8 percent of the world's sea trade, lost more than half its income after attacks on shipping in the Red Sea, although traffic has been coming back.

In November twenty twenty-five, Egypt opened the Grand Egyptian Museum on the edge of Giza, a short distance from the pyramids. All five thousand three hundred and ninety-eight objects from Tutankhamun's tomb are now shown together for the first time, near the pyramid Koofoo built more than 4,500 years ago.
