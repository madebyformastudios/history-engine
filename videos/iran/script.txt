Twenty-five centuries ago, a Persian king ruled the largest empire the world had yet seen, from Egypt to India. The country it grew out of is still on the map. Today we call it Iran.

Iran is a high plateau walled in by mountains, with salt deserts in the middle. It lies between Mesopotamia, Central Asia and India, so almost every army in this part of the world has crossed it.

The first great civilization here was Elam, in the southwest. Susa, later its capital, was first settled around 4200 BC, and by about 3100 BC people there were writing. In the 12th century BC, an Elamite king carried Hammurabi's law code home from Babylonia as loot. That is why the stone was found in Iran.

Sometime in the second millennium BC, new peoples arrived on the plateau, speaking Iranian languages. The Medes settled in the northwest. The Persians settled in the southwest, in a region the Greeks called Persis. That name became Persia.

In 612 BC the Medes helped destroy Nineveh, the capital of Assyria. For a few decades they were the strongest power in Iran, and the Persians were their vassals.

Then, around 550 BC, a Persian king named Cyrus rebelled against the Medes and took their capital. Within eleven years he had conquered Lydia in what is now Turkey, the Greek cities on its coast, and much of Central Asia.

In 539 BC he took Babylon. On a clay cylinder, Cyrus presented himself as a liberator chosen by Babylon's own god. He let deported peoples go home, including the Jews, who returned to Jerusalem to rebuild their temple. The Cyrus Cylinder is sometimes called the first charter of human rights. Most historians see it as royal propaganda in an old Mesopotamian tradition. But the policy was real, and Cyrus was remembered as a just king.

Cyrus died in 530 BC, according to Herodotus, in battle against nomads in Central Asia. His tomb still stands at Pasargadae. His son Cambyses II conquered Egypt in 525 BC.

In 522 BC, a distant relative named Darius took the throne. He claimed he had killed an impostor posing as the king's brother. Many historians suspect Darius himself was the usurper. Either way, he carved his version of events into a cliff at Behistun in three languages. Centuries later, that inscription helped scholars decode cuneiform.

Under Darius, the Achaemenid Empire reached from Egypt and the Balkans to the Indus River in modern Pakistan. He divided it into provinces called satrapies, each paying tribute, and built a royal road of about 2,700 kilometers from Sardis to Susa. Relay riders could carry a message along it in about nine days.

He also began building Persepolis. Clay tablets found there record the rations paid to its workers, men and women alike.

The Greeks saw Persia very differently. Athens had helped the Greek cities of Ionia rebel, so Darius sent an army to punish it. That army lost at Marathon in 490 BC. Ten years later his son Xerxes invaded Greece with a huge force. He won at Thermopylae and burned Athens, but his fleet was crushed at Salamis, and his army was beaten at Plataea the next year.

Greek writers made it a story of survival. Persia lost its foothold in Greece, but the empire lasted another 150 years.

Then came Alexander of Macedon. He crossed into Asia in 334 BC and beat Darius III at Issus and then at Gaugamela in 331 BC. Darius fled east and was murdered by one of his own governors. In 330 BC Persepolis burned. Ancient writers disagree on whether it was a drunken accident or revenge for Athens.

Alexander kept Persian officials, wore Persian clothes and married a Bactrian princess named Roxana. He died in Babylon in 323 BC, at thirty-two.

After Alexander, his general Seleucus took most of Iran.

Around 238 BC, a nomad chief named Arsaces seized the province of Parthia, southeast of the Caspian Sea. His successors, the Parthians, took the whole Iranian plateau and Mesopotamia, and later ruled from Ctesiphon on the Tigris River.

Their weapon was the horse archer. In 53 BC the Roman general Crassus, the richest man in Rome, invaded Mesopotamia. At Carrhae, Parthian archers shot his legions to pieces. About 20,000 Romans were killed and 10,000 captured. Crassus died during a parley, and according to Plutarch his head was used as a prop in a Greek play at the Parthian court.

For the next three centuries, Rome and Parthia fought over Armenia and Mesopotamia. Roman armies sacked Ctesiphon three times, but never held the plateau.

In 224 AD, a local ruler of Persis named Ardashir killed the last Parthian king in battle and founded a new dynasty, the Sasanians. They saw themselves as heirs of Cyrus and Darius, and made Zoroastrianism, Iran's ancient religion, the faith of the state.

His son Shapur I did something no enemy of Rome had ever done. In 260 he defeated the Roman army near Edessa and captured the emperor Valerian alive. Valerian died a prisoner. Shapur had the scene carved into a cliff at Naqsh-e Rostam, next to the tombs of the Achaemenid kings. It is still there.

The Sasanians ruled for more than four hundred years. Under Khosrow I, in the 6th century, books were translated from Greek and Sanskrit, and according to tradition, chess arrived from India.

Then came the last great war with the Byzantine Empire. Starting in 602, the armies of Khosrow II took Jerusalem, conquered Egypt, and in 626 stood across the water from Constantinople. But the Byzantine emperor Heraclius struck back deep into Mesopotamia. In 628, Khosrow was overthrown and executed by his own son.

In the next four years, Persia went through a rapid series of rulers, by some counts more than ten. Both empires had bled each other dry, just as a new power was rising in Arabia.

The Arab armies of the new Muslim state attacked Persia in the 630s. They won at al-Qadisiyyah around 636, took Ctesiphon, and broke the last Sasanian army at Nahavand in 642. The last shah, Yazdegerd III, fled east. In 651 he was killed near Merv. More than a thousand years of empires ruled from Iran had ended.

Iran became Muslim slowly. For two or three centuries much of the population stayed Zoroastrian, Christian or Jewish. Some Zoroastrians left for India, where their descendants are known as the Parsis.

But Persians did not disappear into the new empire. They ran much of its administration. In 750 a revolt that began in Khorasan, in eastern Iran, brought the Abbasid dynasty to power, and the Abbasids built their new capital, Baghdad, about thirty-five kilometers from the ruins of Ctesiphon.

Persian scholars shaped the Islamic golden age. Al-Khwarizmi gave his name to the word algorithm, and Ibn Sina wrote a medical textbook used in Europe for centuries.

The Persian language survived too, now written in Arabic script. Around 1010, the poet Ferdowsi finished the Shahnameh, the Book of Kings, about fifty thousand couplets telling Iran's history from the first mythical kings to the Arab conquest. Iranians still learn it at school.

In the 11th century, Turkish nomads called the Seljuks took over Iran and then Baghdad. They ruled as sultans, with Turkish armies and Persian ministers.

Then the Mongols arrived. In 1219, after a Khwarazmian governor massacred a Mongol caravan and the Shah executed one of Genghis Khan's envoys, Mongol armies swept into eastern Iran. Cities like Nishapur and Merv were destroyed and their people massacred. In 1258, Genghis Khan's grandson Hulagu sacked Baghdad and ended the Abbasid caliphate.

Hulagu's descendants, the Ilkhans, ruled from Iran. In 1295 the Ilkhan Ghazan converted to Islam, and Persian became the language of a court that stretched from Anatolia to Afghanistan.

After the Ilkhanate collapsed in 1335, Timur came. In 1387 the city of Isfahan rebelled against him, and chroniclers wrote that his soldiers built towers from about seventy thousand heads.

In 1501, a fourteen-year-old named Ismail took the city of Tabriz and crowned himself shah. He founded the Safavid dynasty and made Twelver Shia Islam the religion of the state. Most Iranians were Sunni at the time, and conversion was often forced. It is the reason Iran is a Shia country today.

The Safavids' great rivals were the Sunni Ottomans. In 1514, at Chaldiran, Ottoman cannons and muskets crushed Ismail's cavalry. Three years later the same Ottoman sultan, Selim I, conquered Egypt. The border they finally settled in 1639 is close to Iran's western border today.

The greatest Safavid was Shah Abbas I, who ruled from 1587 to 1629. He built a new army with muskets, retook land from the Ottomans, and with English help drove the Portuguese out of Hormuz in 1622. He was also ruthless: he had one son killed and two others blinded because he feared plots.

Abbas moved his capital to Isfahan and built one of the largest city squares in the world. Persians called the city "half the world".

Safavid power collapsed in 1722, when Afghan rebels besieged Isfahan until the starving city surrendered. A soldier from Khorasan named Nader drove the Afghans out and crowned himself shah in 1736. In 1739 he invaded Mughal India and sacked Delhi. His troops killed tens of thousands of people in a single day, and he carried home the Peacock Throne and the Koh-i-Noor diamond, which is now among the British crown jewels. In 1747 his own officers killed him.

After decades of civil war and the rule of Karim Khan Zand, the Qajar dynasty took over and made a small town called Tehran its capital. The Qajars faced a new threat: Russia. After two wars, in the treaties of 1813 and 1828, Iran lost Georgia, Dagestan, and the lands that are now Armenia and Azerbaijan. The Aras River became the border.

To pay their debts, the shahs sold concessions to foreigners. In 1891, after a British company got control of the tobacco trade, Iranians stopped smoking in a nationwide boycott, and the deal was cancelled.

In 1906, after protests and strikes, the shah accepted a constitution and Iran's first elected parliament. A year later, Britain and Russia divided Iran into spheres of influence without asking it. In 1908 the new shah had the parliament shelled by a Cossack brigade with Russian officers.

That same year, a British company struck oil at Masjed Soleyman. The Anglo-Persian Oil Company, later known as BP, took most of the profits. Iran's share was 16 percent.

During the First World War, Iran was neutral, but Russian, British and Ottoman armies fought on its soil anyway. A famine followed that killed around two million people by most estimates.

In 1921, a Cossack officer named Reza Khan seized Tehran in a coup, with encouragement from British officers. In 1925 he made himself shah and founded the Pahlavi dynasty.

Reza Shah built a railway and a modern army. In 1935 he asked foreign governments to stop saying Persia and use the country's own name, Iran. In 1936 he banned the veil, and police pulled headscarves off women in the street.

In 1941, Britain and the Soviet Union invaded Iran to secure its oil and a supply route to the Soviets. They forced Reza Shah to abdicate in favor of his son, Mohammad Reza.

In 1951, the prime minister chosen by parliament, Mohammad Mosaddegh, nationalized Iran's oil. Britain answered with a blockade. On 19 August 1953, a coup organized by the CIA and British intelligence, using paid crowds and loyal army units, overthrew him. In 2013, the CIA released documents confirming its role. Mosaddegh served three years in prison and spent the rest of his life under house arrest.

The Shah now ruled with growing power. His reforms gave women the vote, and the oil boom of the 1970s made Tehran rich. His secret police, SAVAK, tortured opponents. A cleric named Ruhollah Khomeini, exiled since 1964, became the voice of the opposition.

In 1978, strikes and huge protests shut the country down. In January 1979, the Shah left Iran. On 1 February, Khomeini returned to a crowd of millions. On 1 April, after a referendum, Iran became an Islamic Republic. In December, a new constitution placed a Supreme Leader above the elected government.

The revolution had been made by leftists, liberals and Islamists together. Within a few years, Khomeini's followers had crushed the others, executed officials of the old regime and made the veil compulsory. In November 1979, students seized the US embassy and held 52 Americans hostage for 444 days.

In September 1980, Saddam Hussein's Iraq invaded. By 1982 Iran had pushed the Iraqis out, then chose to carry the war into Iraq, sending waves of young volunteers, some of them teenagers, against Iraqi lines. Iraq used mustard gas and nerve agents. The United States, the Soviet Union, France and most Arab states backed Iraq. Syria, Libya and North Korea supplied Iran. In July 1988, the American warship Vincennes shot down an Iranian passenger jet over the Gulf, killing all 290 people on board. The crew said they mistook it for a fighter. The United States expressed regret and later paid compensation.

The war ended in August 1988 with about half a million dead. That same summer, on Khomeini's order, the regime executed thousands of political prisoners. Human rights groups estimate between 2,800 and 5,000.

Khomeini died in 1989, and Ali Khamenei became Supreme Leader. In 2009, millions protested a disputed presidential election and were beaten back.

Iran built up a nuclear program and armed allies from Lebanon to Yemen, and faced international sanctions. A computer worm called Stuxnet, widely attributed to the United States and Israel, damaged its centrifuges, and several nuclear scientists were assassinated in attacks attributed to Israel. A deal in 2015 lifted some sanctions, until the United States walked out in 2018.

In January 2020, an American drone killed the general Qassem Soleimani in Baghdad. Days later, Iran fired missiles at US bases in Iraq, and that night its air defenses shot down a Ukrainian airliner leaving Tehran, killing all 176 people on board. Iran first denied it, then admitted the mistake.

In 2022, a 22-year-old woman named Mahsa Amini died after being arrested by the morality police for her headscarf. The protests that followed, under the slogan "Woman, Life, Freedom", were put down with more than five hundred deaths.

In June 2025, Israel attacked Iran's nuclear and military sites, and the United States joined in by bombing Fordow, Natanz and Isfahan. The war lasted twelve days.

In late December 2025, protests over a collapsing currency spread across the country. In January, security forces opened fire on the crowds. The government admits about three thousand dead. Human rights groups have verified about seven thousand, and other estimates run above thirty thousand.

On 28 February 2026, the United States and Israel launched a new war, with the stated goal of regime change. On the first day, an Israeli airstrike, backed by the United States, killed Ali Khamenei in Tehran. Another hit an elementary school in Minab and killed about 120 children. The US military's own preliminary investigation pointed to an American strike. About a week later, the Assembly of Experts chose Khamenei's son Mojtaba as the new Supreme Leader.

Iran fired missiles at Israel and at Gulf states hosting American bases, and declared the Strait of Hormuz closed. Ceasefires came and collapsed, and in September 2026 American strikes resumed.

Iran today has more than ninety million people. It has been conquered by Greeks, Arabs, Turks and Mongols, and every time, the language and the idea of Iran survived. Ferdowsi's Book of Kings is still read in its schools, and Cyrus's tomb still stands at Pasargadae.
