=== Part 1: Before Persia ===

Twenty-five centuries ago, a Persian king ruled the largest empire the world had yet seen, from Egypt to India. The country it grew out of is still on the map. Today we call it Iran.

Iran is a high plateau walled in by mountains, with salt deserts in the middle. It lies between Mesopotamia, Central Asia and India, so almost every army in this part of the world has crossed it.

The first great civilization here was Elam, in the southwest. Susa, later its capital, was first settled around forty-two hundred BC, and by about thirty-one hundred BC people there were writing. In the twelfth century BC, an Elamite king carried Hammurabi's law code home from Babylonia as loot. That is why the stone was found in Iran.

Sometime in the second millennium BC, new peoples arrived on the plateau, speaking Iranian languages. The Medes settled in the northwest. The Persians settled in the southwest, in a region the Greeks called Persis. That name became Persia.

In six twelve BC the Medes helped destroy Nineveh, the capital of Assyria. For a few decades they were the strongest power in Iran, and the Persians were their vassals.

Then, around five fifty BC, a Persian king named Cyrus rebelled against the Medes and took their capital. Within eleven years he had conquered Lydia in what is now Turkey, the Greek cities on its coast, and much of Central Asia.

In five thirty-nine BC he took Babylon. On a clay cylinder, Cyrus presented himself as a liberator chosen by Babylon's own god. He let deported peoples go home, including the Jews, who returned to Jerusalem to rebuild their temple. The Cyrus Cylinder is sometimes called the first charter of human rights. Most historians see it as royal propaganda in an old Mesopotamian tradition. But the policy was real, and Cyrus was remembered as a just king.

=== Part 2: The First World Empire ===

Cyrus died in five thirty BC, according to Herodotus, in battle against nomads in Central Asia. His tomb still stands at Pasargaday. His son Cambyses the Second conquered Egypt in five twenty-five BC.

In five twenty-two BC, a distant relative named Darius took the throne. He claimed he had killed an impostor posing as the king's brother. Many historians suspect Darius himself was the usurper. Either way, he carved his version of events into a cliff at Bayhistoon in three languages. Centuries later, that inscription helped scholars decode cuneiform.

Under Darius, the Akeemenid Empire reached from Egypt and the Balkans to the Indus River in modern Pakistan. He divided it into provinces called satrapies, each paying tribute, and built a royal road of about 2,700 kilometers from Sardis to Susa. Relay riders could carry a message along it in about nine days.

He also began building Persepolis. Clay tablets found there record the rations paid to its workers, men and women alike.

The Greeks saw Persia very differently. Athens had helped the Greek cities of Ionia rebel, so Darius sent an army to punish it. That army lost at Marathon in four ninety BC. Ten years later his son Xerxes invaded Greece with a huge force. He won at Thermopylae and burned Athens, but his fleet was crushed at Salamis, and his army was beaten at Plateeah the next year.

Greek writers made it a story of survival. Persia lost its foothold in Greece, but the empire lasted another 150 years.

Then came Alexander of Macedon. He crossed into Asia in three thirty-four BC and beat Darius the Third at Issus and then at Gowgameela in three thirty-one BC. Darius fled east and was murdered by one of his own governors. In three thirty BC Persepolis burned. Ancient writers disagree on whether it was a drunken accident or revenge for Athens.

Alexander kept Persian officials, wore Persian clothes and married a Bactrian princess named Roxana. He died in Babylon in three twenty-three BC, at thirty-two.

=== Part 3: Rome's Rival ===

After Alexander, his general Seleucus took most of Iran.

Around two thirty-eight BC, a nomad chief named Arsaseez seized the province of Parthia, southeast of the Caspian Sea. His successors, the Parthians, took the whole Iranian plateau and Mesopotamia, and later ruled from Tesiphon on the Tigris River.

Their weapon was the horse archer. In 53 BC the Roman general Crassus, the richest man in Rome, invaded Mesopotamia. At Karree, Parthian archers shot his legions to pieces. About 20,000 Romans were killed and 10,000 captured. Crassus died during a parley, and according to Plutarch his head was used as a prop in a Greek play at the Parthian court.

For the next three centuries, Rome and Parthia fought over Armenia and Mesopotamia. Roman armies sacked Tesiphon three times, but never held the plateau.

In two twenty-four AD, a local ruler of Persis named Ardashir killed the last Parthian king in battle and founded a new dynasty, the Sasanians. They saw themselves as heirs of Cyrus and Darius, and made Zoroastrianism, Iran's ancient religion, the faith of the state.

His son Shapur the First did something no enemy of Rome had ever done. In two sixty he defeated the Roman army near Edessa and captured the emperor Valerian alive. Valerian died a prisoner. Shapur had the scene carved into a cliff at Nakshe Rostam, next to the tombs of the Akeemenid kings. It is still there.

The Sasanians ruled for more than four hundred years. Under Khosrow the First, in the sixth century, books were translated from Greek and Sanskrit, and according to tradition, chess arrived from India.

Then came the last great war with the Byzantine Empire. Starting in six oh two, the armies of Khosrow the Second took Jerusalem, conquered Egypt, and in six twenty-six stood across the water from Constantinople. But the Byzantine emperor Heraclius struck back deep into Mesopotamia. In six twenty-eight, Khosrow was overthrown and executed by his own son.

In the next four years, Persia went through a rapid series of rulers, by some counts more than ten. Both empires had bled each other dry, just as a new power was rising in Arabia.

=== Part 4: Conquered, Never Erased ===

The Arab armies of the new Muslim state attacked Persia in the six-thirties. They won at al-Kadiseeyah around six thirty-six, took Tesiphon, and broke the last Sasanian army at Nahavand in six forty-two. The last shah, Yazdegerd the Third, fled east. In six fifty-one he was killed near Merv. More than a thousand years of empires ruled from Iran had ended.

Iran became Muslim slowly. For two or three centuries much of the population stayed Zoroastrian, Christian or Jewish. Some Zoroastrians left for India, where their descendants are known as the Parsis.

But Persians did not disappear into the new empire. They ran much of its administration. In seven fifty a revolt that began in Khorasahn, in eastern Iran, brought the Abbasid dynasty to power, and the Abbasids built their new capital, Baghdad, about thirty-five kilometers from the ruins of Tesiphon.

Persian scholars shaped the Islamic golden age. Al-Khwarizmee gave his name to the word algorithm, and Ibn Sina wrote a medical textbook used in Europe for centuries.

The Persian language survived too, now written in Arabic script. Around ten ten, the poet Ferdowsee finished the Shahnameh, the Book of Kings, about fifty thousand couplets telling Iran's history from the first mythical kings to the Arab conquest. Iranians still learn it at school.

In the eleventh century, Turkish nomads called the Seljuks took over Iran and then Baghdad. They ruled as sultans, with Turkish armies and Persian ministers.

Then the Mongols arrived. In twelve nineteen, after a Khwarazmian governor massacred a Mongol caravan and the Shah executed one of Genghis Khan's envoys, Mongol armies swept into eastern Iran. Cities like Neeshapoor and Merv were destroyed and their people massacred. In twelve fifty-eight, Genghis Khan's grandson Hulagu sacked Baghdad and ended the Abbasid caliphate.

Hulagu's descendants, the Ilkhans, ruled from Iran. In twelve ninety-five the Ilkhan Ghazan converted to Islam, and Persian became the language of a court that stretched from Anatolia to Afghanistan.

After the Ilkhanate collapsed in thirteen thirty-five, Timur came. In thirteen eighty-seven the city of Isfahan rebelled against him, and chroniclers wrote that his soldiers built towers from about seventy thousand heads.

=== Part 5: Shahs, Shia and Shrinking Borders ===

In fifteen oh one, a fourteen-year-old named Ismail took the city of Tabriz and crowned himself shah. He founded the Safavid dynasty and made Twelver Shia Islam the religion of the state. Most Iranians were Sunni at the time, and conversion was often forced. It is the reason Iran is a Shia country today.

The Safavids' great rivals were the Sunni Ottomans. In fifteen fourteen, at Chaldiran, Ottoman cannons and muskets crushed Ismail's cavalry. Three years later the same Ottoman sultan, Selim the First, conquered Egypt. The border they finally settled in sixteen thirty-nine is close to Iran's western border today.

The greatest Safavid was Shah Abbas the First, who ruled from fifteen eighty-seven to sixteen twenty-nine. He built a new army with muskets, retook land from the Ottomans, and with English help drove the Portuguese out of Hormuz in sixteen twenty-two. He was also ruthless: he had one son killed and two others blinded because he feared plots.

Abbas moved his capital to Isfahan and built one of the largest city squares in the world. Persians called the city "half the world".

Safavid power collapsed in seventeen twenty-two, when Afghan rebels besieged Isfahan until the starving city surrendered. A soldier from Khorasahn named Nader drove the Afghans out and crowned himself shah in seventeen thirty-six. In seventeen thirty-nine he invaded Mughal India and sacked Delhi. His troops killed tens of thousands of people in a single day, and he carried home the Peacock Throne and the Koh-ee-noor diamond, which is now among the British crown jewels. In seventeen forty-seven his own officers killed him.

After decades of civil war and the rule of Karim Khan Zand, the Kajar dynasty took over and made a small town called Tehran its capital. The Kajars faced a new threat: Russia. After two wars, in the treaties of eighteen thirteen and eighteen twenty-eight, Iran lost Georgia, Dagestan, and the lands that are now Armenia and Azerbaijan. The Aras River became the border.

To pay their debts, the shahs sold concessions to foreigners. In eighteen ninety-one, after a British company got control of the tobacco trade, Iranians stopped smoking in a nationwide boycott, and the deal was cancelled.

In nineteen oh six, after protests and strikes, the shah accepted a constitution and Iran's first elected parliament. A year later, Britain and Russia divided Iran into spheres of influence without asking it. In nineteen oh eight the new shah had the parliament shelled by a Cossack brigade with Russian officers.

That same year, a British company struck oil at Masjed Solaymahn. The Anglo-Persian Oil Company, later known as BP, took most of the profits. Iran's share was 16 percent.

During the First World War, Iran was neutral, but Russian, British and Ottoman armies fought on its soil anyway. A famine followed that killed around two million people by most estimates.

=== Part 6: Oil, Revolution and the Islamic Republic ===

In nineteen twenty-one, a Cossack officer named Reza Khan seized Tehran in a coup, with encouragement from British officers. In nineteen twenty-five he made himself shah and founded the Pahlavi dynasty.

Reza Shah built a railway and a modern army. In nineteen thirty-five he asked foreign governments to stop saying Persia and use the country's own name, Iran. In nineteen thirty-six he banned the veil, and police pulled headscarves off women in the street.

In nineteen forty-one, Britain and the Soviet Union invaded Iran to secure its oil and a supply route to the Soviets. They forced Reza Shah to abdicate in favor of his son, Mohammad Reza.

In nineteen fifty-one, the prime minister chosen by parliament, Mohammad Mossadeg, nationalized Iran's oil. Britain answered with a blockade. On the nineteenth of August nineteen fifty-three, a coup organized by the CIA and British intelligence, using paid crowds and loyal army units, overthrew him. In twenty thirteen, the CIA released documents confirming its role. Mossadeg served three years in prison and spent the rest of his life under house arrest.

The Shah now ruled with growing power. His reforms gave women the vote, and the oil boom of the nineteen seventies made Tehran rich. His secret police, Sahvahk, tortured opponents. A cleric named Ruhollah Khomeini, exiled since nineteen sixty-four, became the voice of the opposition.

In nineteen seventy-eight, strikes and huge protests shut the country down. In January nineteen seventy-nine, the Shah left Iran. On the first of February, Khomeini returned to a crowd of millions. On the first of April, after a referendum, Iran became an Islamic Republic. In December, a new constitution placed a Supreme Leader above the elected government.

The revolution had been made by leftists, liberals and Islamists together. Within a few years, Khomeini's followers had crushed the others, executed officials of the old regime and made the veil compulsory. In November nineteen seventy-nine, students seized the US embassy and held 52 Americans hostage for 444 days.

In September nineteen eighty, Saddam Hussein's Iraq invaded. By nineteen eighty-two Iran had pushed the Iraqis out, then chose to carry the war into Iraq, sending waves of young volunteers, some of them teenagers, against Iraqi lines. Iraq used mustard gas and nerve agents. The United States, the Soviet Union, France and most Arab states backed Iraq. Syria, Libya and North Korea supplied Iran. In July nineteen eighty-eight, the American warship Vinsenz shot down an Iranian passenger jet over the Gulf, killing all 290 people on board. The crew said they mistook it for a fighter. The United States expressed regret and later paid compensation.

The war ended in August nineteen eighty-eight with about half a million dead. That same summer, on Khomeini's order, the regime executed thousands of political prisoners. Human rights groups estimate between 2,800 and 5,000.

Khomeini died in nineteen eighty-nine, and Ali Khamenay became Supreme Leader. In two thousand nine, millions protested a disputed presidential election and were beaten back.

Iran built up a nuclear program and armed allies from Lebanon to Yemen, and faced international sanctions. A computer worm called Stuxnet, widely attributed to the United States and Israel, damaged its centrifuges, and several nuclear scientists were assassinated in attacks attributed to Israel. A deal in twenty fifteen lifted some sanctions, until the United States walked out in twenty eighteen.

In January twenty twenty, an American drone killed the general Kassem Solaymahnee in Baghdad. Days later, Iran fired missiles at US bases in Iraq, and that night its air defenses shot down a Ukrainian airliner leaving Tehran, killing all 176 people on board. Iran first denied it, then admitted the mistake.

In twenty twenty-two, a 22-year-old woman named Mahsa Ameenee died after being arrested by the morality police for her headscarf. The protests that followed, under the slogan "Woman, Life, Freedom", were put down with more than five hundred deaths.

In June twenty twenty-five, Israel attacked Iran's nuclear and military sites, and the United States joined in by bombing Fordo, Natanz and Isfahan. The war lasted twelve days.

In late December twenty twenty-five, protests over a collapsing currency spread across the country. In January, security forces opened fire on the crowds. The government admits about three thousand dead. Human rights groups have verified about seven thousand, and other estimates run above thirty thousand.

On the twenty-eighth of February twenty twenty-six, the United States and Israel launched a new war, with the stated goal of regime change. On the first day, an Israeli airstrike, backed by the United States, killed Ali Khamenay in Tehran. Another hit an elementary school in Meenahb and killed about 120 children. The US military's own preliminary investigation pointed to an American strike. About a week later, the Assembly of Experts chose Khamenay's son Mojtahbah as the new Supreme Leader.

Iran fired missiles at Israel and at Gulf states hosting American bases, and declared the Strait of Hormuz closed. Ceasefires came and collapsed, and in September twenty twenty-six American strikes resumed.

Iran today has more than ninety million people. It has been conquered by Greeks, Arabs, Turks and Mongols, and every time, the language and the idea of Iran survived. Ferdowsee's Book of Kings is still read in its schools, and Cyrus's tomb still stands at Pasargaday.
