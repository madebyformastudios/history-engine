In less than a century, a few nomadic tribes from the Mongolian steppe built the largest connected land empire in history. At its height, it stretched from Korea all the way to Hungary.

And it all began with a boy who had nothing.

This is the story of the Mongol Empire.

Around 1162, a boy named Temujin was born on the steppe of what is now Mongolia. The steppe was divided between rival tribes, who raided and fought each other constantly.

When Temujin was about nine, his father was poisoned by the Tatars, a rival tribe.

His own clan abandoned the family. They survived by fishing, hunting small animals and digging up roots.

As a teenager, Temujin was captured by a rival clan and locked in a heavy wooden collar. One night, he escaped.

He married a young woman named Borte. But soon after the wedding, she was kidnapped by the Merkit tribe.

Temujin gathered allies, including his blood brother Jamukha, and rescued her. It was his first great victory, and people began to follow him.

But Jamukha became his greatest rival. For twenty years, Temujin fought tribe after tribe for control of the steppe.

He crushed the Tatars, the Keraites and the Naimans. In the end, Jamukha was betrayed by his own men and executed.

In 1206, a great assembly of the tribes gave him a new title: Genghis Khan. For the first time, the steppe was united.

Genghis rebuilt Mongol society around the army. Men were organized in units of ten, a hundred, a thousand and ten thousand.

Old tribal loyalties were broken up on purpose. Commanders were promoted for loyalty and skill, not for noble birth.

He introduced a code of law called the Yassa, and a network of relay stations, where riders swapped horses so messages could cross the empire at incredible speed.

Every Mongol warrior was a horse archer, trained from childhood. Each rider brought several spare horses, so an army could cover huge distances without stopping.

First, he turned south. The Tangut kingdom of Western Xia submitted in 1209. Then he invaded the Jin dynasty of northern China.

The Mongols had no experience with walled cities. So they learned, using captured Chinese engineers to build siege weapons.

In 1215, they captured the Jin capital Zhongdu, near modern Beijing.

In the west lay the Khwarazmian Empire, a rich Muslim state in Central Asia. Genghis sent a trade caravan there, but the governor of Otrar had the merchants killed.

Genghis sent envoys to demand justice. The Shah had one of them executed and sent the others back humiliated.

It was a fatal mistake. In 1219, the Mongols invaded. Within three years, Otrar, Bukhara, Samarkand and Merv had fallen.

Cities that resisted were destroyed and their people massacred. Cities that surrendered were usually spared. Word spread fast.

Meanwhile, two generals, Jebe and Subutai, rode all the way around the Caspian Sea, defeating Georgian and Rus armies on the way.

In 1227, Genghis Khan died during a campaign in western China. To this day, nobody knows where he is buried.

His son Ogedei became the Great Khan and built a capital at Karakorum. In 1234, the Jin dynasty was finally destroyed.

Then the Mongols looked west. Between 1237 and 1241, they swept through the Russian principalities, burned Kyiv, and crushed armies in Poland and Hungary.

European knights had never faced anything like this. Mongol riders would pretend to retreat, then turn around and surround their pursuers.

Europe seemed open. But in late 1241, news came that Ogedei had died. The Mongol leaders turned back east, and they never returned in full force.

In the Middle East, Genghis's grandson Hulegu attacked Baghdad in 1258. One of the greatest cities in the world was sacked, and the last Abbasid caliph was killed.

According to legend, so many books were thrown into the Tigris that the river ran black with ink.

But in 1260, the Egyptian Mamluks defeated a Mongol army at Ain Jalut. For the first time, the Mongol advance had been stopped.

By then, the empire was too big to rule from one place. After a civil war between Genghis's grandsons, it broke into four parts: the Golden Horde, the Chagatai Khanate, the Ilkhanate, and the Yuan dynasty in China.

The Yuan was ruled by Kublai Khan. In 1279, he completed the conquest of southern China, and for the first time all of China was under Mongol rule.

He also tried to invade Japan, twice. Both attempts failed. The second fleet was destroyed by a typhoon the Japanese called kamikaze, the divine wind.

Despite all the violence, Mongol rule made the Silk Road safer than ever. Merchants, ideas and travelers like Marco Polo crossed from Europe to China.

But the same routes also carried something deadly. In the 1340s, the Black Death spread from Central Asia to the Black Sea, and from there to Europe.

One by one, the khanates fell apart. The Ilkhanate collapsed in 1335. In 1368, the Yuan were driven out of China by the Ming. The Golden Horde held on the longest, until its grip on Moscow ended in 1480.

The empire was gone. But it had reshaped the map of Asia and Europe, and the countries that rose from its ruins, from Russia to China.

And it all started with a boy on the steppe who refused to give up.
